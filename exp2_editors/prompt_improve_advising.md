TASK. You are given the weights of a language model, Qwen3.5-0.8B (text part: 24 layers of width 1,024; layers 3, 7, 11, 15, 19 and 23 are full-attention layers with 8 query heads and 2 key/value heads of size 256 and a gated output, the other 18 are Gated DeltaNet linear-attention layers with 16 heads of size 128; each layer has a SwiGLU MLP of width 3,584; RMSNorm; vocabulary 248,320; input embedding and output layer tied). Your job is to make it a better college advisor by EDITING ITS WEIGHTS DIRECTLY: find out what goes wrong inside the network when it fails at college-advising questions and repair that with targeted changes.

HOW IT WILL BE MEASURED. After you finish, your edited model and the unedited model are evaluated on a held-out set of college-advising prompts (factual questions about U.S. admissions and financial aid, and student scenarios that state a student's situation) with this fixed protocol: the chat template with thinking disabled (the model answers directly); the user message is the prompt alone; sampling with the model card's non-thinking setting (temperature 1.0, top_p 1.0, top_k 20, presence penalty 2.0) and at most 1,024 new tokens. Rule-graded prompts are scored by whether the answer states the essential facts (regex checks) and avoids stated errors (for example telling a DACA student they are eligible for federal aid, or inventing a dollar figure for the Pell maximum); scenario prompts are also scored by a judge on accuracy, specificity to the student's stated situation, absence of fabricated specifics, and appropriate referral to a human advisor. The config, tokenizer, chat template and decoding settings are fixed: only weight values can change (same tensor names and shapes). You never see the evaluation prompts, but practice items with ids starting ho_ are drawn from the same distribution: you may measure on them, you must never use them to choose an edit.

WHAT COUNTS. We want edits that come from understanding the network: find out what goes wrong when it fails at advising prompts, identify the components responsible (specific neurons, heads, directions or circuits) and change them in a way you can justify with evidence. Repairing a specific failure mechanism is in scope: for example, if the model ignores the student's stated situation (DACA, veteran, parenting, first-generation, transfer) and gives a generic answer, finding what suppresses that information and fixing it counts; so does fixing repetition, premature termination, or a direction that drives a wrong default (such as recommending federal aid to students who are ineligible). Out of scope, and refused by save_edit: the final norm (model.language_model.norm.weight), the input embedding / output layer (model.language_model.embed_tokens.weight, lm_head.weight), and any edit that only multiplies a whole tensor by a constant. Global knobs (making the whole output distribution sharper or flatter) are not what we are looking for, and neither is making answers longer or shorter regardless of the question. Note the published result on a model that does not loop: editing removed obstacles that hid an ability but could not add an ability the model lacked; prefer failures that look like obstacles.

HOW TO WORK.
1. Diagnose first. Look at how the model fails on practice prompts: a missing essential fact, a stated error, an answer that ignores the student's situation, repetition, or being cut off. The generation summary counts these; read some failing texts. Go after the largest category you can explain.
2. Localize causally. That a component is more active on correct answers is not enough. Before relying on a component, show that changing it changes the behaviour you predicted, and try several strengths to see a dose-response.
3. Measure with enough power. Accuracy over n generations has a standard error of about 50/sqrt(n) percentage points. Compare original and edited weights on the SAME prompts with "model": "both"; keep an edit only if its gain is clearly larger than its standard error on prompts that were not used to choose it (the ho_ items are for this). Teacher-forced passes give a dense signal on every token: the log-probability of a correct reference answer, or of a candidate recommendation under contrasting student features, is cheap to measure for hundreds of prompts at once. Edits that change a tensor by a fraction of a percent usually have effects too small to see; test strengths large enough to matter and back off if they hurt.
4. Plan your forward passes: for example one diagnostic generation with activations, teacher-forced passes for dense paired measurements, and a final paired generation ("model": "both") on ho_ items to confirm.
